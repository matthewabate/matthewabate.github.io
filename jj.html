From patient behavior to clinical trial.

Healthcare / Digital Therapeutics  ·  2016 / 7 Months

The Challenge

Three teams were working toward different goals:

Marketing wanted a mobile app that explained blood glucose readings through an avatar. The clinical team saw that as ineffective, but didn't have a clear alternative for a digital solution. IT had a mandate to deliver an AI-driven product within a year.

At the same time, a self-injection device was failing early testing. Children with Type 1 Diabetes were not able to administer a monthly immunotherapy treatment reliably. Errors were frequent and costly.

The work was to understand what was actually happening in both cases and determine what needed to change so each could succeed under real conditions.

What I Did

For the digital product, I led a workshop to align teams on who to focus on. The priority group became patients with the most variable blood sugar levels.

I worked with nurses and behavioral scientists to understand which parts of treatment could be supported digitally, which could be assisted, and which should remain human.

We then conducted ethnographic research with people living with diabetes. We met them in their homes and observed how they actually manage their condition day to day, including where they fall off track.

The goal was to understand whether a digital intervention could change behavior, and if so, what form it would need to take.

In parallel, I synthesized research from the clinical team on children struggling with the injection device. I worked with industrial designers to test and refine new concepts, and led design of a digital version of the instructions for use (IFU).

The Insights

For the digital product, the core issue was not a lack of information. It was that the guidance people were receiving was too difficult to understand, track, and maintain.

Patients did not need more explanation of biological consequences. They needed support in the moments where adherence breaks down.

Effective intervention required three aspects of a behavioral change intervention plan: Problem solving in context, Rapid Realtime Feedback, and Maintenance.

Most of the real work happens between appointments. That is where behavior either holds or slips.

For the injection device, the issue was not user error in isolation. It was the design of the device and instructions under real conditions.

Children struggled because:

  • The device was too large to hold correctly
  • The trigger required more force than they could apply
  • The delay before injection increased anxiety and caused hesitation
  • Instructions were too dense to follow in the moment

In both cases, adherence was not a willpower problem. It was a design problem. Behavior was shaped by environment, context, and cognitive load more than by intention or instruction.

The Solution

For the digital product, we designed a coaching experience built around small, adaptive interventions.

The app delivered personalized challenges based on clinical best practices, adjusted to behavior and context, provided feedback in real time, and connected patients to their care team when needed.

For the injection device, we redesigned both the tool and the instructions.

We created a digital version of the instructions that guided users step by step with a clear hierarchy of information, while remaining compliant with FDA requirements.

We redesigned the device to accommodate a wider range of hand sizes and strengths, reduced the force required to activate it, and adjusted the mechanism to reduce anticipation and stress without compromising performance.

Outcomes

First J&J digital product fast-tracked through the FDA Digital Health Pre-Cert and Breakthrough Devices Programs

First digital instructions for use advanced to clinical trial in the United States

Injection redesign reduced user error by 80 percent and lowered average waste cost by $4,000 per patient annually
